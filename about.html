Professional Introduction

Documents That Work
as Hard as You Do

"A document is not decoration. It is the moment your client decides whether you are worth trusting."

I'm Bayazid Kajol, founder of SBTEXMEDIA — a document design and digital solutions studio built around a single idea: the paperwork a business hands to its customers should be as considered as the product it sells. For more than eight years I have specialised in the unglamorous, high-stakes work that most designers avoid — PDF editing, fillable PDF forms, Google Forms, Microsoft Forms, Jotform, Microsoft Word formatting, Excel templates, and the lead magnet, ebook and workbook design that turns expertise into something a reader will actually finish.

That work has taken me through more than 1,300 completed projects for over 700 clients across the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and much of Western Europe. Clinics, law firms, coaches, schools, agencies, non-profits and manufacturers have all sent me the same brief in different words: this document is costing us time, and we don't know why. Usually the answer is structural, not cosmetic — a form nobody can tab through, a Word file that reflows every time someone opens it, a workbook that asks the reader to think before it has earned their attention.

My job is to find that structural fault and remove it, then make the result look like it belongs to a company worth hiring. I work directly with every client — there is no account manager, no queue, no handoff to a junior. When you brief me, I'm the one who builds it, tests it and answers your questions afterwards.

My Story

How a Broken Form
Became a Career

I did not set out to become a document specialist. Almost nobody does. I started where a lot of designers start — teaching myself Photoshop and Illustrator because I liked the idea of making things that people would look at. The early work was ordinary: social graphics, flyers, small brand marks for local businesses in Bangladesh who needed something better than clip art and could not afford an agency.

The turn came from a job I nearly refused. A client sent me a twelve-page registration pack — the kind of thing a training centre hands to every new applicant — and asked whether I could "make it fillable." It was a scanned document. The typography was inconsistent. Half the fields were drawn as underscores. Applicants had been printing it, filling it by hand, photographing it on a phone and emailing it back. The admin team was retyping every submission by hand and losing roughly a day a week to it.

Rebuilding that pack properly took me far longer than I quoted. I had to learn Adobe Acrobat Pro seriously — not the surface features, but field naming, tab order, calculation scripts, validation, flattening behaviour and how the same file renders in Acrobat versus a phone versus a browser preview. When I handed it back, the applicant could complete it on a phone in four minutes and the admin team's data landed already typed. The client told me it saved them roughly a day of labour every week. That sentence changed what I wanted to do for a living.

It reframed the whole discipline for me. Design is not only what a thing looks like — it is what a thing costs the people who use it. A badly built form charges a tax to every single person who touches it, forever, and nobody ever puts that cost on an invoice. From that point I stopped chasing prettier work and started chasing that hidden tax wherever I could find it: in intake forms, in contracts, in onboarding packs, in course workbooks, in the Excel sheets people had been quietly maintaining by hand for years.

The specialisms stacked from there, each one arriving because a client asked. Fillable PDF forms led to Google Forms and Microsoft Forms when clients wanted the same intake process to live online with data flowing straight into a spreadsheet. Those led to Jotform when the logic got complicated enough to need conditional branching and payment capture. Microsoft Word formatting came from clients who needed the document to stay editable in-house without falling apart. Excel templates came from the same instinct applied to numbers — building a sheet that protects its own formulas so the person using it cannot accidentally destroy it in month three.

Meanwhile the design side never went away; it grew up. Lead magnet design, ebook design and workbook design turned out to be the same problem wearing better clothes. A checklist that nobody completes has failed exactly the way a form nobody can submit has failed. Both are structure problems solved with typography, hierarchy and a genuine respect for the reader's attention.

SBTEXMEDIA is the name I gave that combined practice. It exists because these skills genuinely belong together, and because clients kept telling me how tiring it was to hire one person for the form, another for the design, and a third to fix the Word file — then referee the differences between them. One specialist who understands the document from structure to finish is simply faster, and the result is coherent.

How I Work

My Workflow

The same six steps on a two-hour edit and a two-week workbook. Predictability is the point.

Most bad freelance experiences are not caused by weak skills — they are caused by an absent process. Work disappears into silence, the first draft arrives as a surprise, and the revision round becomes an argument about what was agreed. I follow the same sequence on every project specifically so that none of that happens to you.

1
Discovery & Brief
Before quoting, I ask what the document is genuinely for, who fills it in, what happens to the data afterwards, and which constraints are non-negotiable. This is where most hidden requirements surface — the compliance rule, the legacy software, the colleague who will need to edit it later.
2
Written Scope & Fixed Quote
You receive a written summary of deliverables, file formats, revision rounds and delivery date, with a fixed price. Nothing starts until you confirm it, and nothing outside that scope appears on your invoice later without your approval.
3
Structure First
I build the skeleton before the surface — field logic and tab order for a form, style hierarchy for a Word document, section flow for a workbook. On larger projects you approve this stage before I invest time in visual design, which is what keeps revisions cheap.
4
Design & Build
The visual layer goes on: typography, spacing, colour and brand alignment, plus interactivity, calculations and automation. I work in your brand system where one exists and build a consistent one where it doesn't.
5
Multi-Environment Testing
Every interactive file is tested the way your users will actually meet it — desktop Acrobat, mobile, browser preview, and print where relevant. Forms are submitted with real test data to confirm the output lands correctly at the other end.
6
Delivery, Handover & Aftercare
You receive final files in every format agreed, plus editable sources and a short note on how to maintain the file yourself. Revisions are handled promptly, and I answer questions about a delivered project long after the invoice is closed.

Standards

My Quality Assurance Process

Nothing leaves my desk until it has cleared all six checks. This is the reason the on-time, first-time delivery rate holds.

Quality assurance in document work is not a final glance before sending. It is a set of specific, repeatable checks, because the failures in this field are almost always invisible in a preview — a field that exports under the wrong name, a heading that looks like a heading but isn't tagged as one, a formula that breaks the moment someone inserts a row. I run the same checklist every time, in the same order.

1
Brief Reconciliation
The deliverable is checked line by line against the written scope. Every requested item is confirmed present before anything else is assessed — the most common complaint in freelance work is a missing requirement, not a poor one.
2
Functional Testing
Every field is filled, every checkbox toggled, every calculation verified against manual arithmetic, every conditional rule triggered in both directions. Forms are submitted with test data to confirm the output arrives correctly formatted.
3
Cross-Platform Verification
Interactive PDFs are opened in Acrobat, a mobile viewer and a browser preview, since each renders form fields differently. Word files are checked for reflow. Excel templates are tested against edge-case inputs and empty states.
4
Accessibility & Structure Audit
Reading order, tagged headings, meaningful field labels and tooltips, colour contrast, and keyboard-only navigation from first field to submit. If a document cannot be completed without a mouse, it is not finished.
5
Design & Typography Review
Alignment, consistent spacing, typographic hierarchy, image resolution, bleed and margins for print, and brand accuracy down to exact colour values. This is also the proofreading pass for spelling and grammar.
6
Final Package Check
File names are clean and logical, formats match what was promised, sizes are optimised for email and web, editable sources are included, and the handover note is written. Only then does the delivery go out.
Global Practice

Working With
International Clients

Clients across nine-plus countries and every major time zone — treated as an advantage, not an obstacle.

The large majority of my work comes from the United States, United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, with a steady stream from Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, France and New Zealand, alongside businesses here in Bangladesh. Working across that spread for eight years has taught me that the difficulties people expect from international collaboration are rarely the real ones. Time zones are not the problem. Unclear expectations are.

I operate from Bangladesh (GMT+6), which places my working day ahead of Europe and well ahead of North America. In practice this works in your favour more often than against it: a brief sent at the end of a US business day is frequently answered, and often partly built, before that client's next morning. I keep flexible hours deliberately so that live calls with New York, London or Sydney are straightforward to arrange rather than an ordeal, and I confirm every deadline in your time zone rather than mine, because "Friday" means two different days depending on where you are standing.

Regional conventions matter more in document work than in almost any other design discipline, and getting them wrong reads as carelessness. Date formats differ between US and UK documents. Paper sizes differ — US Letter versus A4 — and a form designed for one and printed on the other will clip. Spelling conventions, currency symbols, address field structures, phone number formats and name-order assumptions all vary. I ask about these at the briefing stage as a matter of routine, and I am equally comfortable working to US or British English throughout.

On the practical side: I communicate in clear professional English by email, WhatsApp, Slack, Zoom or Google Meet, whichever suits your team. I invoice in USD and accept international payment through the standard freelance platforms as well as direct transfer. I sign NDAs and confidentiality agreements without hesitation. Where a client's sector carries specific regulatory obligations — accessibility standards, data-handling rules, retention requirements — I ask what applies to you at the outset and build to it rather than discovering it during a compliance review.

What has kept international clients returning, according to them, is simply reliability: the file arrives when it was promised, in the format that was promised, and it works the first time. Distance stops being relevant the moment that becomes predictable.
