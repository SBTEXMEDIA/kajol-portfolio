Ebook & Workbook Design

Professional Ebook Design: How to Structure and Format a Business Ebook

BK
Bayazid Kajol
Founder of SBTEXMEDIA
Published Sep 8, 2026 12 min read Category Ebook & Workbook Design

Forty minutes of attention is long enough for every layout decision to be felt.

An ebook is the only marketing asset people voluntarily spend forty minutes with. That is a remarkable amount of attention — and it is also the problem, because forty minutes is long enough for every layout decision to be felt. Cramped line spacing that looks acceptable on page two becomes exhausting by page twenty. A heading hierarchy that is nearly consistent stops being navigable somewhere in chapter three.

Good ebook design is mostly about sustaining attention over length. This article covers how to structure a business ebook before designing it, the layout and typography decisions that determine whether it gets finished, how to handle images and data, what changes when a document must work in print as well as on screen, and how workbook material fits alongside it.

If you are still deciding whether an ebook is the right format at all, ebook vs workbook covers that choice. This article assumes the decision is made and deals with producing the thing well.

What Ebook Design Actually Covers

People often mean "make the cover nice". In practice the work divides into four layers, and the visible one matters least:

  • Structure — how the argument is divided, sequenced and signposted.
  • Layout — the grid, margins, and how each page type is composed.
  • Typography — the type system that carries hierarchy across sixty pages.
  • Production — export settings, file size, navigation, accessibility and print readiness.

Weak structure cannot be rescued by good typography. A beautifully set document that is organised badly is still hard to read; a plainly set document that is organised well is not.

Structure Before Design

The most common production mistake is opening a design tool with a finished manuscript and starting on page one. Structure first, and the layout decisions become obvious rather than arbitrary.

Decide the reading pattern

Business ebooks get read in one of two ways, and they need different treatment.

Linear documents build an argument and depend on sequence — a methodology, a market analysis, a strategic case. These tolerate longer prose sections and need strong chapter transitions.

Reference documents get dipped into — a set of techniques, a series of scenarios, a catalogue of approaches. These need aggressive signposting, self-contained sections, and a contents page that functions as a menu rather than a formality.

Deciding which you are producing determines whether a reader can open at page thirty and understand it.

Chunk into sections that fit a spread

A useful discipline: each idea should resolve within roughly one or two pages. Where a section sprawls across six, it usually contains two ideas that want separating. Where three sections each take half a page, they probably want combining.

This is not about arbitrary word counts. It is about giving the reader visible progress — a document where something completes every couple of pages feels shorter than one of identical length that never appears to conclude anything.

Vary the page type deliberately

Plan a rhythm before designing: prose, then a table, then a short case example, then a checklist, then prose again. Uniform pages of body text are what make a PDF feel like homework, and readers abandon them at predictable points.

Page Architecture

A business ebook has a conventional shape, and following it is easier for the reader than inventing something.

The cover

It does two jobs: it appears as a thumbnail wherever the ebook is promoted, and it is page one. The thumbnail duty is the binding constraint — a title that cannot be read at 200 pixels wide will not attract downloads regardless of how it looks full size. Large type, short title, high contrast, and nothing critical near the bottom edge where previews often crop.

Front matter

Keep it brief. A title page, a short statement of who the document is for and what they will be able to do afterwards, and — over roughly eight pages — a contents page. Company history belongs at the back if it belongs anywhere.

Chapter openers

A consistent chapter opening does more for perceived quality than almost any other single decision. It gives the reader a clear breath between sections and makes a long document feel organised. A chapter number, a title, and generous white space is sufficient; it does not need illustration.

The body

Descriptive headings that make sense in isolation, because a large proportion of readers skim before they read. "Why fixed-price quotes fail on scope changes" tells a skimming reader something; "Considerations" does not.

Back matter

A summary or key-takeaways page is the highest-value page in most business ebooks and the one most often omitted. It consolidates the argument into something the reader can act on and refer back to. After that: about the author or business, and one clear next step.

Typography for Long-Form Reading

Over sixty pages, typography stops being aesthetic and becomes ergonomic.

  • Two typefaces at most. One for headings, one for body, both with genuine weight ranges so hierarchy comes from weight rather than from adding a third font.
  • Body text at 10–12pt for a document read on screen at A4. Smaller looks refined in a design tool and fails on a laptop at arm's length.
  • Line height around 1.5. Tight leading is the most common reason a page feels heavy, and the effect compounds over length.
  • Line length of 60–75 characters. Full-width lines on A4 exceed this considerably, which is why generous margins or a two-column grid are not decorative choices.
  • Left-aligned body text. Justification without proper hyphenation produces rivers of white space that get worse in narrow measures.
  • Real contrast between levels. If a subheading is only slightly larger than body text, it is not functioning as a subheading.
  • Restrained emphasis. Bold for genuinely key terms. A page with six bold phrases has none.

Layout and the Grid

A grid is what makes sixty pages look like one document rather than sixty decisions. Even a simple one — a defined margin, a consistent text column, a fixed position for headings and captions — removes most of the drift that makes amateur documents feel unsettled.

The single most underused element is white space. Generous margins and clear separation between sections are what make a document feel considered, and the instinct to cram more onto each page to keep the file short is a false economy. A twenty-page document with room to breathe is read further than a twelve-page document with none.

On column choice: single-column is safer for anything likely to be read on a phone, since two columns force awkward scrolling on small screens. Two columns work well for print-oriented documents and for reference material with short entries.

Images, Data and Callouts

Three categories, with different rules.

Functional images — diagrams, screenshots, process illustrations — earn their space by explaining something the text would take a paragraph to describe. They need to be legible at final size, which usually means simplifying rather than shrinking. A screenshot with eight annotations reproduced at half-page size is unreadable.

Data should be designed, not pasted. A chart exported straight from a spreadsheet arrives with default colours, a redundant legend and axis labels sized for a monitor. Redraw it in the document's own palette, remove everything that is not carrying information, and label directly where possible.

Decorative stock photography is almost always a mistake in business documents. Generic images of people in meetings add pages and subtract credibility. If a page needs visual relief, a pull quote, a callout panel or a well-set table does more.

Callout panels deserve specific mention because they are the most efficient device available: a tinted box containing a warning, a rule of thumb or a worked example breaks up the page, signals importance, and gives skim readers an entry point.

Navigation

Anything over about fifteen pages needs to be navigable, and this is where exported documents most often fall short:

  • PDF bookmarks generated from headings, so the reader can jump between sections in the sidebar.
  • A clickable contents page, linked to the sections rather than merely listing them.
  • Live external links. Every URL clickable — nobody retypes a web address from a PDF.
  • Running headers showing the current chapter, so a reader who opens at page forty knows where they are.
  • Page numbers that match the contents page, including any front matter convention.

Screen, Print and Reflowable

"Ebook" covers three different products, and the differences are not cosmetic.

Most business ebooks are screen PDFs, and should be designed as such. The mistake worth avoiding is designing for print by default — mirrored margins and CMYK conversion on a document nobody will ever print add cost and constrain the design for no benefit.

If a document genuinely needs both, design for print and derive the screen version from it, not the other way round. Going from a print-ready file to a screen file is straightforward; going the other way usually means reworking images that were never captured at sufficient resolution.

Workbook Material Inside an Ebook

Many business ebooks would be more useful with something to complete. Adding exercises changes the reader's relationship to the material from consuming to doing, and a reader who has applied your framework to their own situation is in a different position afterwards.

Three patterns work:

  • Exercises appended at the end of each chapter. Simplest to produce and keeps everything in one file.
  • A separate companion workbook delivered alongside. Cleaner design for both, since the ebook can be dense and the workbook spacious — they are genuinely different layout problems.
  • A summary worksheet at the end, consolidating the reader's conclusions into one page they keep.

If you go this route, design the workbook material properly rather than adding lines under questions. Fields need to be sized for realistic answers, instruction blocks need to be visually distinct from areas the reader writes in, and branding should stay out of the space they are writing in. Where the reader is expected to complete it on screen and send it back, it needs real form fields with sensible tab order rather than a flat page — the mechanics of that are covered in the guide to professional fillable PDF forms.

Export and File Hygiene

Details that have nothing to do with visual design and cause a disproportionate share of problems:

  • File size. Compress images appropriately. Most business ebooks should sit comfortably under 5–10MB; a 40MB file is a poor first impression and may not deliver by email at all.
  • Fonts embedded, so the document renders the same everywhere and can be printed.
  • Document title set in the PDF properties, or the reader's browser tab displays the filename.
  • A sensible filename. care-home-inspection-guide.pdf, not ebook_final_v4.pdf. It sits in their downloads folder for months.
  • Selectable text. A document exported as flat images cannot be searched, copied or read aloud.
  • Tagged structure for accessibility, which also improves how the document behaves when text is copied.

Mobile Readability

A large share of readers open the file on a phone, straight from the delivery email. An A4 page on a phone screen renders everything at roughly a third of its intended size.

The consequences: body text below 10pt becomes unreadable zoomed out; wide tables cannot be read without horizontal scrolling and are better restructured as lists; text set over images loses contrast; and detailed diagrams need to be legible at a third of their size or simplified.

The test takes thirty seconds. Email the PDF to yourself, open it on your phone, and read a page without zooming.

Common Mistakes

  • Designing before the structure is settled, which produces layout decisions that fight the content.
  • Length treated as value. Nobody finishes a sixty-page guide that could have been twenty.
  • Uniform page density from cover to close.
  • Stock photography as filler.
  • Charts pasted straight from a spreadsheet in default styling.
  • No bookmarks or clickable contents in a document long enough to need them.
  • Inconsistent heading levels, chosen by appearance rather than by structure.
  • Exported at print resolution for a document distributed by email.
  • No summary page, leaving the reader with no consolidated takeaway.
  • No next step, so a genuinely useful document leads nowhere.

Conclusion

The decisions that determine whether an ebook gets finished are made before anything is styled: how the material is divided, how each section resolves, and how the reader is signposted through it. Typography and layout then sustain attention across the length, and production settings determine whether the file behaves once it leaves you.

If you are choosing where to spend limited effort, spend it on structure and on the type system. A well-organised document in restrained typography reads well. An ornate one built on weak structure does not.
