Service Overview

Forms People Can Actually
Finish on the First Try

"A form is a conversation. Most bad forms fail because nobody thought about the order of the questions."

SBTEXMEDIA designs professional fillable PDF forms for organisations that need information collected accurately, on any device, without an administrator retyping it afterwards. Led by Bayazid Kajol, founder of SBTEXMEDIA, this service covers everything from a two-page client intake form to a multi-section application pack with automatic calculations, conditional sections and legally-usable digital signatures.

Building a genuinely good fillable form is a structural discipline rather than a design one. Every field needs a sensible internal name so the exported data is readable. The tab order has to follow the visual layout, because administrators fill hundreds of these without ever touching a mouse. Required fields need validation that guides rather than scolds. Totals should calculate themselves. Sections that do not apply should disappear. And all of it must behave identically in desktop Adobe Acrobat, in a phone's PDF viewer and inside a browser preview — three environments that render form fields very differently.

I build in Acrobat Pro at field level, using JavaScript for calculations and validation where it genuinely helps. Where the starting point is a scan or a document with no source file, the PDF editing work comes first so the form is built on clean foundations. And where a process would genuinely be better online, I will tell you so — sometimes Google Forms or Jotform is the right answer, and recommending the wrong tool to win a job helps nobody.

The Real Cost

Why Fillable PDF Forms
Matter

A badly built form charges a tax to every single person who touches it — and that cost never appears on an invoice.

Almost every business runs at least one process on a form that does not work properly. The registration pack that applicants print, complete by hand, photograph on a phone and email back. The order sheet where the totals are added manually and occasionally wrong. The consent form that gets returned three times because a required field was easy to miss. None of these are dramatic failures, which is precisely why they survive for years.

The cost is real and measurable, though. Every hand-completed form that arrives as an image has to be retyped by someone, and every retyping introduces a chance of error. A team processing forty submissions a week at four minutes of manual data entry each is losing well over two hours weekly to a problem that a properly-built form eliminates completely. Multiply that across a year and the form has quietly cost more than any professional would have charged to fix it.

Then there is the abandonment cost, which is harder to see because the people it affects never contact you. A form that will not open on a phone, that resets when the respondent taps the wrong thing, or that demands information in a confusing order simply does not get completed. You do not receive a complaint — you receive nothing at all, and you have no way of knowing how many enquiries you lost to it.

There is also a compliance dimension that catches organisations out. In regulated sectors, a form is a record. If it cannot be completed by someone using a screen reader or navigating by keyboard alone, that is an accessibility failure. If a signature field is decorative rather than a real signature field, the document may not hold up when it is questioned. If exported data arrives with field names like Text1 and Untitled5, nobody can audit it later.

A properly engineered fillable PDF removes all of it. The respondent completes it on whatever device they have, guided by validation and logical order. Totals calculate themselves. Signatures are genuine. The data arrives structured, named and ready to use. And because the file is built to be maintained, next year's version is a small edit rather than another rebuild.

How It Works

Our Process

The same six steps on a one-page consent form and a forty-page application pack.

Form projects fail in a specific way: everyone agrees on the fields, nobody discusses what happens to the data afterwards, and the finished form turns out to export something the client's system cannot read. My process front-loads those questions deliberately, so the awkward discoveries happen during the brief rather than after delivery.

1
Discovery & Data Mapping
Before quoting I ask who completes the form, on what device, what happens to the data afterwards, and which fields are legally required. This is where the real requirements surface — the system that needs a specific CSV column order, the regulator who mandates particular consent wording.
2
Written Scope & Fixed Quote
You receive a written summary listing every field, the logic and calculations involved, the export format, revision rounds and a delivery date, at a fixed price. Nothing starts until you approve it.
3
Document Preparation
The underlying document is cleaned or rebuilt first — scans are OCR'd, spacing is corrected, and field areas are sized to fit realistic answers rather than optimistic ones. A form built on a messy base is always a fragile form.
4
Field Engineering
Fields are created with meaningful internal names, then wired up: validation rules, formatting masks, dropdown values, calculations, conditional show-and-hide behaviour, signature fields, and a tab order that follows the visual reading order exactly.
5
Multi-Environment Testing
The form is completed end to end in desktop Acrobat, on iOS and Android, and in browser preview. Every calculation is verified against manual arithmetic, every conditional rule triggered in both directions, and a real test submission exported to confirm the data lands correctly.
6
Delivery, Handover & Aftercare
You receive the finished form, a flattened print version where useful, the editable source, and a short note on updating fields yourself. Revisions are handled promptly and I answer questions long after the invoice closes.
