Service Overview

Downloads People Actually
Finish

"An unopened download is not a lead. It is an email address attached to a person who has already forgotten you."

SBTEXMEDIA designs lead magnets for businesses that want their free download to do actual work. Led by Bayazid Kajol, founder of SBTEXMEDIA, this service covers checklists, cheat sheets, guides, research reports, swipe files, fill-in templates, printable planners, resource libraries and article-specific content upgrades — all designed around a single measure of success: whether the person who downloads it uses it.

That measure matters more than most people realise, because the opt-in is not the goal. The goal is the relationship that follows it, and that relationship only forms if the download delivers something. A twenty-eight-page PDF that nobody opens produces an email address belonging to someone who has already forgotten who you are — and who marks your next email as spam. A two-page checklist that solves a real problem in ten minutes produces someone who now believes you are useful. Those are radically different outcomes from identically-shaped conversion metrics.

So I design for completion, not for volume. That means being ruthless about length, front-loading the useful part rather than building up to it, using structure and hierarchy so the reader can see the whole thing is manageable, and giving fill-in space that is genuinely usable. It also means the finished piece must look like it came from a business worth trusting — because it is often the first thing a prospect ever receives from you. Where the download needs to be interactive, it can be built as a fillable PDF form; where it is long enough to be a book, see ebook design.

The Real Cost

Why Lead Magnet Design
Matters

You do not have a lead generation problem. You have a "nobody opens the thing" problem, and it is invisible.

Almost every business measures the wrong half of this. You can see your opt-in rate, so you optimise the landing page, the headline, the button colour. What you cannot see is what happens after the download link is clicked — whether the file was opened, whether it was read past page two, whether anyone acted on it. That invisibility is why so many businesses have a lead magnet that technically converts and a list that never buys anything.

The most common cause is simply length. Somebody decides that more value means more pages, so a useful idea becomes a forty-page guide. The recipient opens it, sees the scroll bar, decides they will read it properly at the weekend, and never does. Meanwhile the equivalent two-page checklist would have been completed in one sitting and pinned above their desk. Perceived effort is what determines whether a download gets used, and length is the loudest signal of effort there is.

Structure is the second failure. Many lead magnets bury the useful part behind three pages of introduction, author background and context-setting. The reader has already given you their email — they do not need to be sold to any more, they need the thing they were promised. Front-loading the payoff and putting the credentials at the back consistently changes completion rates, and it costs nothing to do.

Then there is the credibility problem. Your lead magnet is frequently the first thing a prospect ever receives from your business — the first physical artefact of what working with you is like. If it arrives as default template typography with a stretched logo and inconsistent spacing, it tells them something about your standards before you have said a word. Fairly or not, that judgement transfers directly to your paid work.

Finally, most lead magnets are built once and never revised, because nobody owns the editable file. The designer used a tool the client cannot access, or supplied a flat PDF only. Two years later the offer has changed, the branding has moved on, and the download is quietly embarrassing — but replacing it means starting over. Supplying editable sources as standard removes that entirely.

How It Works

Our Process

The same six steps on a one-page checklist and a twenty-page research report.

The step that changes outcomes most is the one clients least expect: I will often recommend cutting the content down. It feels counterintuitive to pay for design and be told there should be less of it, but perceived effort is what determines whether a download gets used — and a shorter piece that gets completed beats a comprehensive one that gets archived.

1
Audience & Offer Alignment
Before quoting I ask who this is for, what they are struggling with, what you sell afterwards, and what format that specific audience actually uses. A download that does not connect to what comes next generates subscribers who were never going to buy.
2
Written Scope & Fixed Quote
You receive a written summary covering format, page count, deliverable file types, revision rounds and a delivery date, at a fixed price. Nothing starts until you approve it.
3
Content Shaping
Your material is restructured for completion — the payoff moved to the front, sections sequenced so momentum builds, and length trimmed where trimming helps. I will always explain the reasoning, and it remains your call.
4
Design & Brand Application
Typographic hierarchy, spacing, colour and imagery built on your brand where one exists and a consistent system where it does not. Fill-in areas are sized for real handwriting, and a cover or preview image is produced for your opt-in page.
5
Screen & Print Testing
Checked on phone and desktop for readability, and test-printed on both A4 and US Letter to confirm margins, bleed and fill-in space survive contact with a real printer. File size is optimised so email delivery does not fail.
6
Delivery & Editable Sources
You receive the print and web-optimised PDFs, the preview graphics for your landing page, and the editable source files — so you can update the download yourself when your offer changes rather than commissioning it again.
